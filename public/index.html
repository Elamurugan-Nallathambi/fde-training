Where an FDE fits in the AI stack

AI runs on six layers. Most of the money goes into the bottom layers; most of what makes one company's AI different from another's is built in the top two. That is where the FDE works: choosing a model, then building the agent and observability layers so the client gets AI that fits, is safe, costs as little as possible, can be maintained and does what it was set up to do.

  1. Observabilitywatch, evaluate, governFDE builds here

    Records every step the system takes, scores the quality of its answers, catches problems before users do, and keeps the audit trail a regulator or an unhappy customer will ask for. Small layer, big consequences.

    LangSmith, Langfuse, Braintrust, Arize, Datadog, OpenTelemetry

  2. Agentdecide, call tools, actFDE builds here

    The logic around the model: routing, tools and their permissions, memory, retrieval, approvals and the loop that turns a model into something that does real work. Where the client's solution actually lives.

    Claude Agent SDK, OpenAI Agents SDK, Google ADK, LangGraph, Microsoft Agent Framework, MCP

  3. Modelweights and inferenceFDE chooses here

    The models themselves, plus the serving that turns them into answers at a price per token. Changes fastest and gets cheaper fastest.

    Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek, Qwen; served by them or by clouds and hosts

  4. Computewho runs the machines

    Buys the chips, builds the data centers and rents capacity by the hour, or runs it privately. Where most of the money is spent.

    AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle Cloud, CoreWeave, your own data center

  5. Siliconwho makes the chips

    Designs and manufactures the processors, memory and networking that everything else runs on. The hardest layer to enter.

    NVIDIA, AMD, TSMC, Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron; in-house chips such as Google TPU and AWS Trainium

  6. Energypower, grid, cooling

    Electricity, transmission and cooling for data centers. Now a real limit on how fast the rest can grow.

    Utilities and grid operators, power generators

What a good solution looks like

  • SuitableThe right kind of AI for this job, or no AI where rules do better
  • SecureClient data protected, access limited, every action traceable
  • Least costSmallest model and fewest calls that still meet the measure
  • MaintainableSomeone else can run, fix and change it after you leave
  • Does the jobMeasured against the outcome it was set up to deliver

How an FDE works with a client

Every engagement, and every module in this program, follows the same loop: from "how does this work actually happen" to "a running solution the owner trusts".

  1. EmbedJoin the client team, meet the process owner and the people doing the work.
  2. DiscoverInterview, shadow, read documents and data; map the real process.
  3. FrameWrite the problem, the success measure and the business case.
  4. DesignLay out two or three options and recommend one.
  5. BuildShip a POC on realistic data with AI coding assistants.
  6. EvaluateTest on real cases; fix what fails; set human review.
  7. Demo and hand overShow the value, agree the next step, leave a runbook.

Who this program is for

Anyone who wants to use today's AI well: effectively, securely and at the lowest cost. That takes two things. You learn how the underlying pieces work (models, data, APIs, networks, security), and you learn how to read an industry so you know where AI really helps. Everyone starts from one of two places.

Track A

Software and digital background

You can build. The program adds domain and business analysis, discovery, product ownership and communication. Fast-track Module 3 after a skills check.

Track B

Domain background

R&D, quality, regulatory, clinical, supply chain, manufacturing or operations. You know the work. The program adds code basics, APIs, data, AI-assisted building and deployment. Fast-track Module 4 after a skills check.

Learners work in mixed pairs, one from each track, so they teach each other and finish the final project together.

How your progress is checked

Three layers of evidence, so a learner, a mentor and a manager can all see the same progress.

1. Every step: the GitHub journal

In the tutor, each of the 13 steps saves evidence to the learner's own GitHub repository and must pass defined checks. A manager opens one link to see what is done.

2. Checkpoints with a mentor

  1. Week 2: knowledge check on LLM concepts and a prompt log review
  2. Week 4: track skills check: a small coding task (domain track) or an industry brief (software track)
  3. Week 6: recorded discovery role-play with a mock client
  4. Week 10: POC demo with evaluation results
  5. Week 12: final review panel: demo, questions, hand-over pack

3. Rubric and graduation

The panel scores seven dimensions as not yet, practitioner or FDE-ready. To graduate: FDE-ready in discovery, build quality, evaluation and safety, and communication, and at least practitioner in the rest.

How each week runs

Every week follows the same rhythm, so learners always know what is expected and when. Read each lane left to right: it shows who does what across the week.

How we teach

  • Learn by doing: every week ends with something handed in and committed
  • Real material: messy documents, realistic data, real constraints
  • AI as tutor and tool: use it to explain, draft and code, then show your own reasoning
  • Evidence over opinion: every claim backed by an interview, a document or data
  • Feedback every week from a mentor and from peers

Who supports you

  • Program lead: owns cohorts, the plan and graduation decisions
  • Mentors: experienced engineers and consultants, one per four to six learners
  • Mock clients: domain experts who play the client in discovery, demos and the final project
  • Reviewers: score hand-ins and the final project against the rubric
